About

My name is Juliette Faivre, and I am French, Polish, and Luxembourgish. From a young age, I have lived in more than seven countries, and I enjoy discovering new cultures, learning languages, and discussing new ideas. Technology was one of the things that tied those places together for me as a child.

As I studied management from 2018–2022, I embraced technology's power to create new products, increase productivity, and expand what people can build. At the same time, I became increasingly interested in the labor and wider societal effects of technology. That pushed me to look at technology development and deployment differently: through incentives, metrics, and more humane design. During that time, I also got involved in science and technology think-tank research through TTCSP at the University of Pennsylvania.

In 2022, I went on to complete a master's in Technology Policy at Cambridge. This coincided with generative AI and large language models emerging, which quickly became the focus of my research projects.

I happen to like butterflies. If you catch one, perhaps it will tell you more about me.

Research

Interests

AI policy and governance asks how institutions, rules, standards, incentives, and practices shape the development and deployment of AI.

Because the field moves so quickly, my interests evolve with the ideas I read and discuss. The thread tying them together is generally: which existing institutions can help us govern AI, and what needs to change or be created to support safer development?

In this context, I have studied and continue to study the following areas:

  • Socioeconomic impacts of AI, especially labor and creative markets, digital sovereignty, and broadly shared benefits.
  • Accountability, bias, and transparency, including enforceable frameworks for high-stakes systems and alignment.
  • Online safety and privacy, including AI companions, deepfakes, and CSAM.
  • International coordination, regulatory fragmentation, and geopolitics in AI policy.

As a result, I draw from policy, management, engineering, law, ethics, philosophy, and geopolitics.

Approach

My research is mixed-methods. I have a strong qualitative background in literature reviews, semi-structured interviews, and policy/document analysis. I also use quantitative methods from the social sciences, with experience in SPSS and foundational training in Python and R. I like trying to make the method follow the policy question.

Taxing Artificial Intelligence
arXiv working paper · with Sarah H. Cen · 2026
📰 Read the CMU news article about our paper. 💡
Abs

While AI promises major benefits, its development and deployment can shift costs onto others, including environmental pressures on local communities, labor and creative displacement, and systemic risks from rapid frontier development. Taxation is an integral part of policy design, and recent academic, industry, and policy debates have begun to consider whether tax instruments can help address these harms. In this paper, we explore the viability of AI taxation. More broadly, AI taxation should not be understood only as Pigouvian correction. In the AI context, taxation can also correct harmful activity, redistribute unevenly borne costs and gains, and fund regulatory capacity. We discuss the main externalities associated with AI and survey possible tax instruments, including corporate income and rent-based taxes, consumption taxes on AI-related services, and excise instruments tied to specific AI activities. We further assess the benefits and pitfalls of these instruments, including feasibility, measurement problems, incidence, leakage, and innovation costs. Because AI externalities differ in nuanced ways, tax policy must be carefully designed and matched to the specific harms and policy objectives.

The AI Act: towards global effects?
SSRN working paper · 2023
Abs

This paper examines the European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act (AIA) and its potential to become a global gold standard for trustworthy AI. The AIA aims to address ethical concerns and ensure responsible AI development. The paper highlights the need for AI guidelines, discusses the key features of the AIA, and explores controversies surrounding the legislation, particularly regarding newer generative AI technologies. It also discusses the ongoing discussions and potential for a "Brussels effect," where the AIA's standards influence global AI regulation. The paper concludes by emphasizing the importance of a regulation that sets guardrails for AI while promoting understanding and collaboration with the private sector.

Negotiations in tech: an analysis of asymmetric ransomware negotiations
SSRN working paper · 2023
Abs

Ransomware attacks have emerged as a significant threat to both private and public organizations, with devastating consequences in terms of data encryption and financial loss. This paper delves into the realm of asymmetric ransomware negotiations, focusing on the power dynamics between cybercriminals and their corporate victims. We assume a targeted ransomware attack scenario, where cybercriminals invest efforts in selectively distributing ransomware to specific victims. Drawing on concepts from literature, we analyze the "unlevel playing field" created by cybercriminals through illegitimate strategies and unethical means. Additionally, game theory analysis reveals the strategic interactions between both parties, highlighting factors influencing ransom demands and the victim's willingness to pay. Furthermore, we explore the role of social engineering tactics employed by cybercriminals to coerce and manipulate victims during negotiations. By examining the psychological aspects of communication, we uncover the dynamics of fear and urgency utilized by cybercriminals to drive victim behavior. We also discuss countermeasures that victims can employ to mitigate the effects of social engineering and maximize their interests in negotiation. Ultimately, this paper aims to shed light on the intricacies of asymmetric ransomware negotiations, providing insights for better understanding and responding to this growing cybersecurity threat in the technology domain.
